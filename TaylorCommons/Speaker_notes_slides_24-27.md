# Speaker notes: slides 24 to 27 (Steph)

## Slide 24: The March 2025 Refinancing Trade
So this is where things really started going wrong at Taylor Commons. In March 2025, the sponsor decided to trade
our cheap 3.57% fixed-rate agency loan for a $16.5 million bridge loan at about 9.5%. The goal was to move the land
into an HFC so the property wouldn't pay property taxes.

Here's the math. The exemption saved about $490 thousand a year in taxes. But the new loan added over a million
dollars of interest, and the HFC charges its own fees on top of that. So even while the exemption was working, the
trade lost about $600 thousand a year.

Interest coverage went from about 1.9x to under 1x, which means the property isn't earning enough to cover its own
interest.

And it gets worse. The exemption is ending at the end of this year, so we keep the expensive debt and lose the one
thing we got for it. Even if it never ended, we took on so much interest that the trade still didn't work. We'll
get to the takeaways later, but this really shows how bad a decision it was.

## Slide 25: HB 21: What Ends and What Stays
So why is the exemption ending? Two months after the refinancing, Texas passed HB 21. It ends this kind of HFC tax
break at the end of 2026 unless the city of Fort Worth approves the deal. Fort Worth backed the law, so we don't
think that's happening. There's a lawsuit, but it hasn't gone anywhere.

The left side is the important part: only one thing changes on January 1st, and it's the tax break. Taxes go back
to roughly $520 thousand a year. Everything else stays: the 99-year ground lease, the HFC fees, the rent rules, and
the fact that we can't leave without the HFC's permission.

So we keep all the strings and lose the benefit. That leaves about $940 thousand of NOI against roughly $1.5 million
of interest, with the loan coming due in March.

## Slide 26: The 2027 Cliff and Refinancing Gap
And that March maturity is the cliff. The $16.5 million loan has to be paid off or refinanced.

On about $940 thousand of NOI, a new lender would only give around $9.4 million on the tightest test, so there's a
$7.1 million hole. Even if the exemption somehow survived, there's still a $2 million gap.

So there are really three ways this goes. Someone puts in about $7 million of new money, W Financial agrees to
extend again, or the property gets sold or handed back to the lender. The building is worth less than the loan, so
nobody's putting in new equity. Our view is that this property goes back to the lender at maturity.

## Slide 27: Valuation and Recovery
So what's it actually worth, and does SMU get anything back?

The two dashed lines are what matters: the $16.5 million loan, and the loan plus the preferred equity at about
$21.5 million. Our common equity only has value to the right of that second line.

Without the tax break, which is our base case, the property is worth $13 to $16 million. That doesn't even cover
the loan. Even the fund's $19.2 million mark, which assumes the exemption survives, and the sales comps fall short of
the loan plus the pref.

On the right, in a sale today the lender takes about 84 cents on the dollar, and the pref and the common, including
our $350 thousand, get nothing. For the common to see a dollar, the property would need about $1.45 million of NOI,
roughly 46% more than it earns today.

So SMU's $350 thousand is at zero. That's the capital structure side. The other half of the story is that the
business plan meant to grow that NOI never really happened, which is the renovation plan, up next.
